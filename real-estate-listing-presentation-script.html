The listing presentation, and how to practise it

You get one sitting at someone's kitchen table, usually against two other agents, and the seller has already decided what their house is worth. The agent who wins is rarely the one with the glossiest folder — it's the one who can hold a conversation without looking down.

Cut it into beats, and give each one a job

Don't rehearse a monologue. Split the presentation into six to eight beats and write one line under each saying what it should do to the seller, not what it says. Something like: earn the right to disagree about price. Make the marketing feel concrete rather than generic. Make the commission feel like an investment rather than a fee. Get a decision today rather than a think-about-it.

There's evidence behind this. Research on how actors actually learn scripts (Noice & Noice) found they rarely memorize by rote — they break the text into beats and attach an intention to each. Rehearse the doing and the words follow. In their studies, non-actors coached to use the material on a listener out-recalled deliberate memorizers, while people who only analyzed the text did worse than rote learners. The intent to communicate is the active ingredient.

It also gives you a recovery cue. If you lose your thread at a kitchen table, you probably won't remember the sentence, but you will remember what that beat was for — and you can talk your way back.

Three things must be verbatim

Most of the presentation should be gist-solid and conversational. Three parts should be word-perfect, because they're the parts that get quoted back to you and the parts where hesitation reads as evasion:

  • Your commission answer. The single most predictable question you will ever be asked, and the one agents most often fumble. If it comes out as "well, it depends, usually around…" you have just invited a negotiation you didn't need to have.
  • Your pricing rationale. The comps, the numbers, and the sentence that connects them to your recommendation.
  • Your close. The exact words you use to ask for the signature.

Everything else can breathe. Those three should be reflexive.

Rehearse the price conversation, not the price slide

The listing appointment usually turns on one exchange: the seller's number is above yours. You cannot win that with a chart. You win it by having said the words out loud, calmly, thirty times before — including the version where they push back twice.

Practising under mild evaluative pressure genuinely inoculates against choking (Beilock & Carr, 2001) — the arousal stops being a novel threat and becomes a familiar one. So don't just know your answer to "the agent from the other firm said he'd list at four-fifty." Say it aloud, under a timer, into a recorder, until it comes out level.

Why re-reading your folder does nothing

Reviewing your listing packet before the appointment feels like preparation and mostly builds false confidence. In the testing-effect studies (Roediger & Karpicke), material people kept retrieving was near 80% a week later; material they only restudied fell to the mid-30s. Retrieval is what builds the memory; reading builds the feeling.

But cold recall on day one fails too, because there's nothing there yet. The method proven on freshly-learned material is diminishing cues (Fiechter & Benjamin, 2018): recall with the full text visible, then with words blanked, then from first letters, then from nothing. Each rung forces retrieval while keeping enough success to be worth doing.

Practise out loud, ideally walking

Speaking aloud beats silent reading by 10–20% (the production effect), and a listing presentation is a spoken performance — rehearse in the modality you'll perform in. Movement helps further: in Noice & Noice's experiments people who rehearsed with purposeful movement recalled 31% versus 19% for rote memorizers. Twenty minutes walking the neighbourhood, presenting out loud to an imaginary seller, is worth more than an hour with the folder.

Then have someone be difficult

Sellers interrupt. They mention the neighbour who sold in a week. They ask what you'll do differently in the first fourteen days, and they ask it while you're mid-sentence about photography. The first time you handle that should not be at the table.
